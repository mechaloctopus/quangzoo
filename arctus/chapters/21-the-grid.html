Analect Twenty-One

The Grid

"The most common way people give up their power is by thinking they don't have any."

Every centralized system has a single point of failure. A power grid serves millions — and when it goes down, millions go dark simultaneously. A global supply chain feeds billions — and when it breaks, as it did in 2020, shelves empty overnight. This is not conspiracy theory. It is systems engineering. Any network that routes all traffic through a central node is, by definition, fragile. The internet was designed as a distributed network for exactly this reason: no central server, no single point of failure, no way to take it all down at once. The DARPA engineers understood in the 1960s what most people still haven't grasped: resilience requires decentralization.

The Trap

Modern life is built on dependency. Most people depend entirely on external systems for every basic need: electricity from a centralized grid, water from municipal treatment, food averaging 1,500 miles farm-to-plate, heat from pipelines or grid power, communication from a handful of telecom companies, currency from centralized banking, information from a few platform companies. Each dependency is a chain. Most of the time invisible. The moment any system falters, the chains become visible and the individual discovers they are entirely at the mercy of systems they do not control, did not design, and cannot repair.

The Framework

  • I
    Redundancy. More than one source for every critical need. Grid goes down? Solar and batteries. Store empties? Garden and stored food. Bank freezes? Cash and tangible assets.
  • II
    Decentralization. Energy from the sun on your roof, not exclusively from a plant 200 miles away. Food from your garden and neighbors, not exclusively from a warehouse across the country.
  • III
    Local production. Shorter supply chain = more resilient. Food grown in your yard can't be disrupted by a shipping crisis in another hemisphere.
  • IV
    Skill diversification. The sovereign individual can grow food, repair systems, generate energy, purify water, provide first aid, navigate without GPS. Each skill is a backup system for a dependency that might fail.
  • V
    Community networks. Sovereignty is not isolation. The strongest resilience is found in networks of sovereign individuals supporting each other. The mycorrhizal model: each tree independent, the forest connected underground.

Energy

Solar PV has dropped ~99% in cost since 1976. A residential system with battery storage can provide most or all of a household's electricity. Combined with efficiency measures, grid dependency approaches zero. The argument against centralized grids is structural, not ideological: millions of miles of transmission lines are vulnerable to weather, equipment failure, and cyber attack. A distributed model — every rooftop a power plant, every garage a battery — has no single point of failure.

Information

The sovereign mind cross-references. Reads primary sources. Applies The Razor to every claim regardless of source. Maintains a personal library — physical books that cannot be remotely edited, deleted, or algorithmically suppressed. The consolidation of information into a few platform companies is a centralized chokepoint. Diversify sources. Read across the political spectrum, across national boundaries, across disciplines.

However combinations or associations may now and then answer popular ends, they are likely to become potent engines by which cunning, ambitious, and unprincipled men will be enabled to subvert the power of the people.

— George Washington, Farewell Address (1796)

The Practical Path

Sovereignty Gradient

Year 1: Start a garden. Install a water filter. Build 30-day food supply. Reduce debt.

Year 2: Expand garden. Learn preservation. Solar assessment. Build practical skills. Strengthen neighbor networks.

Year 3: Install solar + battery if feasible. Rain catchment. Diversify income. Build physical reference library.

Ongoing: Each year, identify the single greatest remaining dependency and create a backup.

The paradox: individual sovereignty makes you a better community member. The person who can feed themselves can also feed their neighbor in crisis. You can't give from an empty cup. Put your own oxygen mask on first. Then help.

"The most common way people give up their power is by thinking they don't have any."

— Alice Walker

Fortify yourself. Then fortify your neighbor. That is the way.
