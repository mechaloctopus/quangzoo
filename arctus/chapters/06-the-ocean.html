Analect Six

The Ocean

"The eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me."

There's a moment — sometimes in meditation, sometimes in crisis, sometimes in the gap between one breath and the next when you've forgotten to maintain the illusion — when the boundary dissolves. The line you've drawn your entire life between "me" and "everything that is not me" just vanishes, like a chalk line on a sidewalk in the rain. Nothing changes externally. Everything changes internally. The separation was never real. It was a useful operating assumption — a map drawn on water — and the water just erased it.

This is nonduality. Not a philosophy you adopt on Tuesdays. Not a belief you subscribe to like a streaming service. A direct recognition of what was already the case before you learned to slice the world into subject and object, self and other, this side and that side. Every contemplative tradition that has ever gone deep enough points here. The language is different. The finger is different. The moon is the same.

A human being is part of the whole, called by us 'Universe,' a part limited in time and space. He experiences himself, his thoughts and feelings, as something separated from the rest — a kind of optical delusion of his consciousness. This delusion is a kind of prison for us, restricting us to our personal desires and to affection for a few persons nearest to us.

— Albert Einstein, Letter to Robert S. Marcus (1950)

That's not a mystic talking. That's the guy who cracked the geometry of spacetime. When the greatest physicist of the 20th century describes the separate self as an "optical delusion" and calls it a "prison," maybe it's worth investigating what the contemplatives have been saying about this for three thousand years.

The Wave and the Ocean

Here's the simplest metaphor and probably the truest one: you are a wave. You have a shape, a direction, a speed, a height. You are distinct from the wave next to you. You can be described, measured, photographed. You are, by every conventional definition, an individual thing.

And yet you are made of nothing but ocean. You arose from it. You will return to it. At no point during your entire existence were you ever composed of anything other than the same water that composes every other wave. Your individuality is real at one level of description and completely illusory at another. Both levels are true simultaneously. This is not a contradiction. It is a parallax — the same reality seen from two different angles.

The Indian tradition of Advaita Vedanta ("non-dual end of the Vedas") makes the case with surgical rigor. Adi Shankara, around 800 CE: Brahman alone is real. The world is appearance. The self is nothing but Brahman. You are not the body. Not the thoughts. Not the emotions. Not the story you tell about yourself at parties. You are the awareness in which all of those arise and dissolve — the screen on which the movie plays. The screen is untouched by the drama. It was there before the movie started. It'll be there when the credits roll.

You are not a drop in the ocean. You are the entire ocean in a drop.

— Rumi

Vipassana — Seeing It Directly

If this codex recommends one practice above all others for direct contact with what this chapter is pointing at, it's Vipassana meditation as taught in the tradition of S. N. Goenka. Not because it's the only valid practice — there are many — but because it's the most systematic, the most empirical, and the most ruthlessly focused on observation rather than belief. It doesn't ask you to believe anything. It asks you to sit still and look.

A standard Vipassana course is ten days. Silence. No phones, no books, no writing, no eye contact. You wake at 4 a.m. and meditate for roughly ten hours a day. The instruction is simple: observe the breath. Then systematically scan the body from head to feet, feet to head, noticing every sensation — tingling, pressure, warmth, pain, pleasure — without reacting to any of it. Just observe.

The first three days are typically brutal. The mind screams. The back aches. Every impulse to escape, distract, analyze, or scroll is stripped away. By day four or five, something shifts. The sensation field changes. What was solid and fixed becomes subtle and flowing. The meditator begins to directly perceive anicca — impermanence — not as a philosophy but as a felt fact in the body. Every sensation arises and passes. The boundary between "body" and "air" softens. The inside/outside distinction that seemed so absolute reveals itself as a convention, not a reality.

These courses are offered worldwide, entirely by donation. Free. The only cost is ten days and the willingness to sit with yourself in silence. It might be the most important ten days you ever spend.

Aikido — Nonduality in Motion

If Vipassana is nonduality sitting still, Aikido is nonduality throwing people across the room. Founded by Morihei Ueshiba (1883–1969), Aikido is a martial art built on a radical premise: there is no opponent. The attacker and defender are a single system. The attack is not resisted but redirected — the defender enters into the attacker's movement, blends with it, and guides it to resolution. Your force and my awareness become one movement.

Ueshiba's insight: conflict arises from the illusion of separation. If I believe you're truly "other," your gain is my loss and violence becomes logical. If I recognize we're expressions of the same force, harming you is harming myself — and the intelligent response to your attack is not counter-attack but integration. This is the Golden Rule in martial form. This is the Mirror turned into a throw.

The art of peace is not easy. It is a fight to the finish, the slaying of evil desires and all falsehood within.

— Morihei Ueshiba, The Art of Peace

The Shared Mind

Nonduality makes a claim that sounds extreme until you think about it carefully: we share a mind. Not telepathy. Not woo. Something more obvious and more radical: the language you think in, the concepts you use, the categories you perceive through — none of these originated in you. They were inherited, absorbed, breathed in from a cultural atmosphere that was itself inherited from prior generations stretching back to the first hominid who pointed at fire and made a sound that another hominid understood.

Your "original" thought is a particular node in a vast network through which that thought found its expression. Newton and Leibniz independently invented calculus at the same time. Darwin and Wallace independently arrived at natural selection. The ideas were ready to emerge. The individuals were the antennae.

The nondual realization doesn't dissolve individuality. It contextualizes it. You are both the unique wave and the whole ocean. The wave has its own shape, its own trajectory, its own irreplaceable beauty. But it's made of nothing other than ocean. Recognizing this doesn't diminish the wave. It reveals its true depth.

Where does the wave end?

Living It

Nonduality is not an escape from the world. It is the deepest possible engagement with it. When the boundary between you and the world dissolves, there's nowhere to escape from and nowhere to escape to. There's only this — the living, breathing, unbearable, inexhaustible present moment. And that turns out to be more than enough.

The practices that support this recognition are simple (not easy, simple):

  • Vipassana — Observe the body. See impermanence directly. Watch the boundaries soften.
  • Self-inquiry — Ask "Who am I?" and follow the question inward. Not seeking an answer but dissolving the questioner. (Ramana Maharshi's method.)
  • Presence — Be here. Fully. In whatever you're doing. Dishes, walking, talking. When the mind wanders, notice and return. (Thich Nhat Hanh's entire teaching.)
  • Nature — Spend time in wild places. The forest doesn't know where it ends and you begin. Sit still long enough and neither will you.

"We are not human beings having a spiritual experience. We are spiritual beings having a human experience."

— Pierre Teilhard de Chardin

Sit with this. Not as a concept to collect. As a door. Walk through it. The other side isn't somewhere else. It's exactly where you are — seen, finally, without the filter.
