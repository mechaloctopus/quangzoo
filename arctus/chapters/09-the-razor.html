Analect Nine

The Razor

"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool."

Robert Anton Wilson opened Prometheus Rising with a challenge most people will never accept: believe nothing. Not because truth doesn't exist, but because the act of believing — of locking a conclusion in place and defending it against all comers — is the precise moment you stop learning. Belief is the bolt that locks the prison door from the inside. The key is always within reach. You just have to be willing to not know.

Critical thinking is not cynicism. It's not the teenager's reflexive "everything's a lie." It's the disciplined art of holding ideas at arm's length — examining them carefully, weighing the evidence for and against, checking for hidden assumptions, and then assigning a provisional degree of confidence that stays open to revision when new data shows up. The difference between "I believe this" and "Based on available evidence, this appears to be the case, and I remain open to being wrong" is the difference between a fortress and a laboratory. Live in the lab.

Belief is the death of intelligence. As soon as one believes a doctrine of any sort, or assumes certitude, one stops thinking about that aspect of existence.

— Robert Anton Wilson, Cosmic Trigger I (1977)

The Hierarchy of Evidence

Not all evidence is created equal. Learning to weigh evidence by reliability is the single most practical skill in this entire chapter:

LevelTypeWeight
1Systematic reviews & meta-analyses of RCTsHighest
2Randomized controlled trialsVery high
3Cohort studies (large groups, over time)High
4Case-control studiesModerate
5Expert opinionLow
6Anecdote ("my buddy tried it and...")Nearly zero

Notice: expert opinion ranks near the bottom. Not because experts are dumb, but because expertise without rigorous evidence is still opinion in a lab coat. History is a graveyard of expert consensus that was catastrophically wrong — stomach ulcers "caused by stress" (actually bacteria), continental drift "impossible" (now the foundation of geology), dietary fat "the enemy" (actually it was sugar the whole time).

The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool.

— Richard Feynman, Caltech Commencement (1974)

The Bugs in Your Brain

Your brain is not a truth-detecting machine. It's a survival organ shaped by millions of years of evolution to make fast decisions in dangerous environments. Many of the shortcuts it developed were brilliant on the savanna and actively dangerous in a world of complex information:

Cognitive Biases You Need to Know

Confirmation bias — You seek out and overweight information that confirms what you already believe. This is the most dangerous bias because it makes every other bias invisible.

Dunning-Kruger effect — The less you know, the more confident you feel. The more you know, the more doubt you have. (See: The Backbone.)

Availability heuristic — You overestimate the probability of vivid, recent events. Plane crashes feel common; car crashes feel routine. Car crashes kill orders of magnitude more people.

Sunk cost fallacy — You keep investing in a losing course because you've already invested so much. The rational move is to evaluate only future costs and benefits. The past is spent regardless.

Anchoring — The first piece of information you receive disproportionately influences everything after it. The first price quoted. The first diagnosis. The first headline.

Awareness of bias is itself a form of inoculation — not perfect, but significant. The critical thinker doesn't claim to be bias-free. The critical thinker actively hunts for bias in their own reasoning and builds systems — peer review, devil's advocacy, pre-mortem analysis — to catch what their brain will inevitably miss.

The Steel Man

The opposite of a straw man — where you weaken your opponent's argument to make it easy to knock down — is the steel man: you strengthen your opponent's argument to its most powerful possible form before you try to refute it. If you can't beat the strongest version, you haven't beaten anything. You've just beaten a scarecrow and called it victory.

Daniel Dennett's version of this (paraphrasing Anatol Rapoport): "You should attempt to re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says, 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way.'" Practice this and you will discover two things: first, your opponents are often smarter than you assumed; second, occasionally, their position is actually right — which is the most valuable possible outcome, because it means you just learned something.

How to Evaluate Any Claim

  • 1
    Source — Who's making the claim? What's their expertise, track record, and potential bias?
  • 2
    Evidence — What data supports it? Anecdotal, observational, or experimental? How large is the sample? Has it been replicated?
  • 3
    Mechanism — Is there a plausible explanation for how the claimed effect works? Correlation without mechanism warrants caution.
  • 4
    Consistency — Does it fit with established knowledge? Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. (Sagan's razor.)
  • 5
    Falsifiability — Can the claim, in principle, be proven wrong? If no possible observation could contradict it, it's not science. It's dogma wearing a lab coat.
  • 6
    Cui bono? — Who profits if you believe this? Follow the incentive. You'll often find the distortion.

The Paradox of Uncertainty

Here's the part most people flee from and the critical thinker learns to inhabit: you can never be completely certain about anything empirical, and that's the correct state. Certainty belongs to mathematics and formal logic. The moment you step into the real world — the world of facts and events and claims about reality — certainty becomes impossible. The best you can do is assign degrees of confidence based on the weight of evidence. And that is enough.

The person who says "I'm 95% confident based on current evidence, and willing to revise" is more honest, more accurate, and ultimately more reliable than the person who says "I'm 100% certain." The first person can update when surprised. The second has to defend their ego against reality. Reality always wins that fight. It just takes longer than you'd hope.

"The whole problem with the world is that fools and fanatics are always so certain of themselves, and wiser people so full of doubts."

— Bertrand Russell

Think. Question. Weigh. Revise. And never mistake the comfort of certainty for the achievement of truth. They have never been the same thing.
