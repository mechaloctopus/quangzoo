Chapter Five

Nonduality

"The eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me."

There is a moment — sometimes in meditation, sometimes in crisis, sometimes in the gap between one breath and the next — when the boundary dissolves. The line you have drawn your entire life between "me" and "everything else" simply vanishes, the way a wave realizes it was always the ocean. Nothing changes externally. Everything changes internally. The separation was never real. It was a useful fiction — a map drawn on water.

This is nonduality. Not a belief, not a philosophy you adopt, but a direct recognition of what was already the case before you learned to divide the world into subject and object, self and other, inside and outside. Every contemplative tradition points toward it. The language differs; the territory is the same.

The eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me; my eye and God's eye are one eye, one seeing, one knowing, one love.

— Meister Eckhart, Sermon 12 (c. 1300)

The Illusion of Separation

From the moment of birth, consciousness begins drawing lines. This is mother. This is not-mother. This is my hand. That is the wall. This is pleasure. That is pain. These distinctions are biologically necessary — a newborn that cannot distinguish nipple from blanket will not survive. But what begins as survival mechanism calcifies into metaphysics. By adulthood, most people live inside a total conviction that they are a separate entity — a self contained inside a bag of skin, looking out at a foreign universe through two small windows.

The nondual traditions say: that conviction is the fundamental error. Not the distinctions themselves — those remain useful at the practical level — but the belief that they reflect an ultimate truth. The wave is distinct from other waves. But it is not separate from the ocean. The distinction is real; the separation is illusory.

You are not a drop in the ocean. You are the entire ocean in a drop.

— Rumi (attributed, from various collections)

Albert Einstein, not typically cited as a mystic, wrote a letter to a grieving father that captures the nondual insight with striking precision:

A human being is part of the whole, called by us 'Universe,' a part limited in time and space. He experiences himself, his thoughts and feelings, as something separated from the rest — a kind of optical delusion of his consciousness. This delusion is a kind of prison for us, restricting us to our personal desires and to affection for a few persons nearest to us. Our task must be to free ourselves from this prison by widening our circle of compassion to embrace all living creatures and the whole of nature in its beauty.

— Albert Einstein, Letter to Robert S. Marcus (1950)

Advaita Vedanta — Not Two

The most rigorous philosophical expression of nonduality comes from the Indian tradition of Advaita Vedanta — literally "non-dual end of the Vedas." Its greatest exponent, Adi Shankara (c. 788–820 CE), argued that only Brahman — infinite, unchanging, undivided consciousness — is ultimately real. The world of multiplicity that we perceive is maya — not illusion in the sense of nonexistence, but illusion in the sense of misidentification. You see the rope and mistake it for a snake. The rope is real. The snake is the error of perception.

Brahman alone is real. The world is appearance. The self is nothing but Brahman.

— Adi Shankara, Vivekachudamani (c. 800 CE)

In practical terms: you are not the body, not the thoughts, not the emotions, not the story you tell about yourself. You are the awareness in which all of these arise and dissolve — the screen on which the movie plays. The screen is untouched by the drama. It was there before the movie started and it remains when the film ends. Recognizing yourself as the screen rather than the character — that is liberation (moksha).

Vipassana — Seeing Things as They Are

Vipassana means "insight" or "clear seeing" in Pali. It is the meditation practice most directly associated with the historical Buddha, and it remains one of the most powerful technologies for directly experiencing nonduality — not as an idea, but as a felt reality in the body.

The practice, as taught in the tradition of S. N. Goenka (1924–2013), is deceptively simple: sit still. Close the eyes. Observe the breath. Then systematically scan the body from head to feet and feet to head, noticing every sensation — tingling, pressure, warmth, pain, pleasure — without reacting to any of it. Just observe.

What happens over ten days of sustained practice (the standard Vipassana retreat is ten days of silence) is remarkable. The meditator begins to directly perceive what the Buddha called anicca — impermanence. Every sensation, no matter how solid it seems, arises and passes away. Pain that felt permanent dissolves. Pleasure that felt stable shifts. The body reveals itself not as a solid object but as a field of continuously changing sensations — a process, not a thing.

This body is not me; I am not caught in this body. I am life without boundaries. I have never been born and I have never died.

— Thich Nhat Hanh, No Death, No Fear (2002)

As the practice deepens, the boundary between "my body" and "the air around my body" begins to blur. The sensations in the hand become indistinguishable from the sensations where the hand meets the air. The inside/outside distinction softens. Not as hallucination, but as accurate perception — you are literally seeing what was always there: a unified field of sensation in which the borders are drawn by habit, not by nature.

This is the science of the self — not believed but observed. Vipassana asks nothing of faith. It asks only that you sit still long enough to see clearly.

Equanimity — The Balance Point

The fruit of nondual practice is equanimity (upekkha in Pali) — the ability to remain balanced in the face of any experience, pleasant or unpleasant, without grasping at one or pushing away the other. This is not apathy, not numbness, not detachment in the cold sense. It is the deepest form of engagement: being fully present to what is, without needing it to be different.

Equanimity is the emotional expression of nonduality. When you no longer experience yourself as separate from the flow of life, there is nothing to defend, nothing to acquire, nothing to fear losing. You can face the storm with open hands because you are not only the boat — you are also the storm, and the ocean it rides on, and the sky above.

Peace comes from within. Do not seek it without.

— Siddhartha Gautama, The Buddha

Aikido — Nonduality in Motion

If Vipassana is nonduality sitting still, Aikido is nonduality in motion. Founded by Morihei Ueshiba (1883–1969) — known to practitioners as O-Sensei — Aikido is a martial art built on a radical premise: there is no opponent.

In Aikido, the attacker and the defender are understood as a single system. The attack is not resisted but redirected — the defender enters into the attacker's movement, blends with it, and guides it to a resolution that neutralizes the violence without destroying the attacker. The technique is not "my force against your force" but "your force and my awareness becoming one movement."

The art of peace is not easy. It is a fight to the finish, the slaying of evil desires and all falsehood within. On occasion the voice of peace resounds like thunder, jolting human beings out of their stupor.

— Morihei Ueshiba, The Art of Peace

Ueshiba's insight was that conflict arises from the illusion of separation. If I believe you are truly "other," then your gain is my loss, and violence becomes logical. But if I recognize that we are expressions of the same force, then harming you is harming myself — and the intelligent response to your attack is not counter-attack but integration. This is the Golden Rule in martial form.

The Shared Mind

Nonduality makes a claim that sounds extreme until you examine it carefully: we share a mind. Not in the supernatural sense of telepathy, but in the structural sense that the language you think in, the concepts you use, the categories you perceive through — none of these originated in you. They were inherited, absorbed, breathed in from a cultural atmosphere that was itself inherited from prior generations stretching back to the first hominid who pointed at fire and made a sound that another hominid understood.

Your thoughts are not your thoughts. They are thoughts — impersonal patterns moving through the medium of your consciousness the way weather moves through the atmosphere. The medium is local; the weather is global. When you have an "original" idea, you are a particular node in a vast network through which that idea found its expression. Newton and Leibniz independently invented calculus at the same time. Darwin and Wallace independently arrived at natural selection. The ideas were ready to emerge; the individuals were the channels.

The individual has always had to struggle to keep from being overwhelmed by the tribe. If you try it, you will be lonely often, and sometimes frightened. But no price is too high to pay for the privilege of owning yourself.

— Friedrich Nietzsche (attributed, often paraphrased)

The nondual realization does not dissolve individuality — it contextualizes it. You are both the unique wave and the whole ocean. The wave has its own shape, its own trajectory, its own beauty. But it is made of nothing other than ocean. Recognizing this does not diminish the wave; it reveals its true depth.

Where does the wave end and the ocean begin?

All Past Lives Are Everyone's Past Life

If nonduality is true — if the separation between self and other is indeed a useful fiction rather than an ultimate truth — then the concept of "past lives" takes on a radical new meaning. It is not that your individual soul transmigrated through a sequence of bodies. It is that the consciousness appearing as you has always been the same consciousness appearing as everyone.

Every life ever lived was "your" life — because there is only one life appearing as many. The pharaoh and the slave. The saint and the murderer. The mother and the child. All of it was — is — the single awareness wearing different masks, playing different roles, experiencing itself from every possible angle. This is the deepest meaning of compassion: you have been the person you are judging. You have been the person you are helping. You have been everything.

If the doors of perception were cleansed, every thing would appear to man as it is — Infinite.

— William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (1793)

Living Nonduality

Nonduality is not a concept to believe. It is a recognition to fall into. The practices that support it are simple — not easy, but simple:

  • 1
    Vipassana meditation — Observe the body, sensation by sensation, without reacting. See impermanence directly. See the boundaries dissolve.
  • 2
    Self-inquiry — Ask "Who am I?" and follow the question inward. Not seeking an answer but dissolving the questioner. This is the method of Ramana Maharshi.
  • 3
    Presence — Practice being fully here, now, in whatever you are doing. Washing dishes, wash dishes. Walking, walk. When the mind wanders to past or future, notice it and return. This is the teaching of Thich Nhat Hanh.
  • 4
    Aikido or Tai Chi — Move the body in practices designed to dissolve the boundary between self and force, between action and response.
  • 5
    Nature immersion — Spend time in wild places. The forest does not know where it ends and you begin. Sit still long enough and neither will you.

Nonduality is not an escape from the world. It is the deepest possible engagement with it — because when the boundary between you and the world dissolves, there is no longer anything to escape from or to. There is only this — the living, breathing, inexhaustible present moment, which turns out to be more than enough.

"We are not human beings having a spiritual experience. We are spiritual beings having a human experience."

— Pierre Teilhard de Chardin

Sit with this. Not as a concept to add to your collection. But as a door. Walk through it. The other side is not somewhere else. It is exactly where you are — seen, finally, without the filter of separation.
