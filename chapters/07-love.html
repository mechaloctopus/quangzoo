Chapter Seven

Love

"Your task is not to seek for love, but merely to seek and find all the barriers within yourself that you have built against it."

The Greeks had at least seven words for love. Modern English has one, and it is asked to do the work of all seven - to describe the bond between mother and infant, the fire between lovers, the loyalty between soldiers, the reverence between devotee and divine, the quiet affection between old friends, the delight in beauty, and the unconditional compassion for all beings. Compressing all of these into a single syllable is like compressing the entire electromagnetic spectrum into the word "light." Technically correct. Practically blind.

This chapter expands the word. Not to be academic, but because the precision of your vocabulary determines the precision of your experience - as the previous chapter on language demonstrated. When you can distinguish between the kinds of love available to you, you can cultivate each one deliberately, rather than stumbling between them hoping for the best.

Your task is not to seek for love, but merely to seek and find all the barriers within yourself that you have built against it.

- Rumi (attributed, from various collections)

The Seven Forms

  • I
    Eros - Erotic love. The fire. The gravitational pull between bodies. Eros is the force that Plato described in the Symposium as a daemon - neither god nor mortal, but a bridge between the two. It begins in the body and, when followed with awareness, can lead to the most transcendent experiences available to human consciousness. Eros is not merely sexual; it is the desire for union - the drive to dissolve the boundary between self and other. It is the matter-confidence discussed in the chapter on confidence: warm, magnetic, instantaneous. Dangerous when mistaken for the whole of love. Sacred when understood as the gateway.
  • II
    Philia - Deep friendship. The love between equals who share values, experiences, and mutual respect. Aristotle considered philia the highest form of human relationship - higher than eros, because it is based not on need or attraction but on shared commitment to the good. A true friend is, in Aristotle's phrase, "a single soul dwelling in two bodies." Philia is the love that sustains you through decades, that shows up at 3 a.m. without being asked, that tells you the truth when flattery would be easier.
  • III
    Storge - Familial love. The bond between parent and child, between siblings. Storge is the love you did not choose - it was given to you by the accident of birth. It can be the deepest comfort and the deepest wound, often simultaneously. The work of storge is learning to love the people you did not select, with all their imperfections, and allowing them to love your imperfect self in return.
  • IV
    Agape - Unconditional love. The love that expects nothing in return. In the Christian tradition, agape is the love of God for creation - total, unearned, inexhaustible. In the Buddhist tradition, it maps to metta - loving-kindness extended to all beings without discrimination. Agape does not depend on the worthiness of the recipient. It flows because it is the nature of the source to flow, the way the sun shines on the just and unjust alike.
  • V
    Ludus - Playful love. The flirtation, the banter, the dance of early attraction. Ludus is the lightness that keeps relationships alive after the initial fire of eros settles into embers. Without ludus, relationships become business arrangements. With it, they remain adventures.
  • VI
    Pragma - Enduring love. The love that has survived time, conflict, boredom, betrayal, and the slow revelation that the other person is not who you imagined them to be - and choosing to stay anyway. Pragma is not the absence of passion; it is passion that has been tested by reality and found durable. It is the love of couples married fifty years who still hold hands - not because they feel a constant surge of emotion, but because they have built something real together and they know its value.
  • VII
    Philautia - Self-love. Not narcissism - which is the absence of genuine self-love, compensated by the performance of self-importance - but the quiet, honest appreciation of your own being. You cannot give what you do not have. The person who does not love themselves cannot love others without distortion - their love becomes a transaction, a plea for validation, a bargain. Healthy philautia is the foundation from which all other loves flow.

Love is composed of a single soul inhabiting two bodies.

- Aristotle (attributed)

Love as Physics

Strip the poetry away and love reveals itself as a force - as real as gravity, as measurable as electromagnetism, as fundamental to the structure of reality as any equation in physics. Consider:

Atoms bond. Electrons share orbits. Molecules attract. Cells cooperate. Organisms form symbiotic partnerships. Colonies emerge. Societies organize. Civilizations build. At every scale of existence, the pattern is the same: separate entities move toward union. The universe, it appears, has a bias toward connection. Physicists call it the strong nuclear force, the electromagnetic force, gravity. Biologists call it symbiosis, mutualism, pair bonding. Mystics call it love. The word is different. The pattern is the same.

Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, the Jesuit paleontologist and philosopher, argued that love is not a human invention but a cosmic force - the same force that drives atoms to form molecules, molecules to form cells, cells to form organisms, and organisms to form societies. He called it "the most universal, the most tremendous, and the most mysterious of the cosmic forces." From this perspective, when you love another person, you are not doing something uniquely human. You are participating in the fundamental activity of the universe.

Love alone is capable of uniting living beings in such a way as to complete and fulfill them, for it alone takes them and joins them by what is deepest in themselves.

- Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, The Phenomenon of Man (1955)
The force that moves through all things

The Neuroscience of Connection

Love is not merely metaphor in the brain. It is chemistry, circuitry, and measurable change:

Oxytocin - the "bonding hormone" - is released during physical touch, eye contact, orgasm, breastfeeding, and acts of trust. It reduces cortisol, lowers blood pressure, and literally calms the amygdala (the brain's fear center). When you hug someone for twenty seconds, both brains are being chemically rewired toward safety and connection (Uvnas-Moberg, 2003).

Mirror neurons - discovered in the 1990s by Giacomo Rizzolatti's team - fire both when you perform an action and when you observe someone else performing it. They are the neurological basis of empathy - the literal mechanism by which one nervous system resonates with another. When you see someone in pain, your brain activates the same regions as if you were in pain. We are wired for each other. Isolation is not the natural state; connection is.

Vagal tone - the health of the vagus nerve, discussed in the chapter on breath - is both a predictor and a product of social connection. People with high vagal tone form more positive social connections. And positive social connections increase vagal tone. The loop is self-reinforcing: love generates the capacity for more love.

The longest-running study of adult development in history - the Harvard Study of Adult Development, begun in 1938 and still ongoing - has followed hundreds of individuals from adolescence into old age. Its single most important finding, as summarized by current director Robert Waldinger: "Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period." Not wealth. Not fame. Not achievement. Relationships. The quality of your connections is the single strongest predictor of your health, happiness, and longevity.

The good life is built with good relationships.

- Robert Waldinger, TED Talk (2015), summarizing the Harvard Study of Adult Development

The Practice of Love

Erich Fromm, in The Art of Loving (1956), argued that love is not a feeling but a practice - a discipline as demanding as any art. "Love is an activity, not a passive affect," he wrote. "It is a standing in, not a falling in." The person who waits passively for love to arrive like lightning is confusing eros (which does arrive unbidden) with agape, philia, and pragma (which must be built, day by day, through acts of attention, care, knowledge, and responsibility).

Fromm identified four elements of the practice of love:

Fromm's Elements of Love

Care - Active concern for the life and growth of the other. Love without care is empty sentiment.

Responsibility - Not obligation but response-ability: the willingness to respond to the needs of the other, freely chosen, not imposed.

Respect - From Latin respicere, to look at. To see the other as they actually are, not as you wish them to be. Respect is the antidote to the projection that turns love into possession.

Knowledge - Not information but penetrating knowledge - the deep, ongoing effort to understand the other from the inside, to move beyond surfaces into the center of their being.

Love, practiced this way, is the most demanding and most rewarding discipline in human life. It asks you to see clearly (critical thinking), to be present fully (stillness), to give generously (the golden rule), to hold lightly (nonduality), and to show up consistently (the twelve codes). Every chapter in this book converges here. Love is not one subject among many. It is the subject to which all others are ultimately in service.

Love and Nonduality

The nondual traditions teach that separation is an illusion. If that is true, then love is not the creation of a connection where none existed - it is the recognition of a connection that was always there. When you love another person, you are not bridging a gap. You are seeing through the illusion of the gap. The wave recognizes itself in another wave and remembers: we are both the ocean.

Out beyond ideas of wrongdoing and rightdoing, there is a field. I'll meet you there.

- Rumi, Masnavi (trans. Coleman Barks)

This is why love feels like homecoming - not like arriving somewhere new, but like returning to somewhere you have always been. The lover does not create love. The lover removes the barriers to love that fear, ego, habit, and distraction have erected. Rumi's instruction is precise: your task is not to seek for love, but to find and dismantle the walls you have built against it.

Dismantle them. One by one. Starting now. Starting with yourself.

"Where there is love there is life."

- Mahatma Gandhi
